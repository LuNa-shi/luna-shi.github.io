## TL;DR

An agent is a running execution unit configured by a Task, an Environment and a Capacity. Its design precedes its execution, while its history develops through observation, action and feedback. TEC uses these three primitives to reinterpret topology, roles, communication, memory and automatic agent design. Its implications concern how the parts constrain one another: more inference cannot distinguish cases hidden by every available view; successful subtasks need a sound composition rule to establish the parent goal; and the critical path belongs to a chosen execution plan, which new capacities or reusable results can change. A system can improve through shared knowledge while individual capacities stay fixed. Capacity and Environment changes can also have value only in combination. Finally, when a system changes its own task generation or verification, higher internal scores need not mean better performance on the original objective.

## 1. We cannot say why multi-agent systems fail

Three years of frameworks—CAMEL's role-play (Li et al., 2023), MetaGPT's standard operating procedures (Hong et al., 2023), ChatDev's virtual company (Qian et al., 2023), AutoGen's programmable conversations (Wu et al., 2023), and the orchestrator-plus-specialists pattern now shipping in every coding assistant—have produced a body of negative results that is, by now, the most solid empirical knowledge the field has. Cemri et al. (2025) annotated over 1,600 traces from seven open-source systems and found failure rates between 41% and 87%, in fourteen modes across three families: the task was under-specified, the agents disagreed about what they were doing, or nobody checked the answer. Kapoor et al. (2024) showed that accuracy-only leaderboards rewarded agents that simpler and cheaper baselines dominated. Multi-agent debate often fails to outperform simpler single-agent baselines despite using more inference compute (Zhang et al., 2025g), and a single agent with a good prompt matches the best discussion protocols (Wang et al., 2024a). Practitioners split on the same line: Anthropic reports that parallel sub-agents pay for themselves on breadth-first research (Anthropic, 2025a); Cognition advises against multi-agent designs for most software work because fragmented context yields incoherent output (Cognition, 2025).

None of this is evidence against multi-agent systems. It is evidence that we describe them in the wrong vocabulary. We talk about roles, orchestrators, planners and critics—the _forms_ of organization—and the forms say nothing about the structure of the task or about who can see what. The older field of distributed AI never made this mistake: the contract net matched task structure to node capability (Smith, 1980); the blackboard was a design for which knowledge source sees which partial result (Erman et al., 1980); the Dec-POMDP was a proof that coordination cost is a function of information fragmentation, NEXP-complete in the worst case (Bernstein et al., 2002). Language models made agents cheap and, in the process, severed that lineage. What follows is an attempt to restore it, starting from a claim about what an agent _is_.

## 2. The spine: an agent's essence precedes its existence

In _Existentialism Is a Humanism_, Sartre distinguished two kinds of thing. A paper-knife is conceived by an artisan who has its use and its method of manufacture in mind before it exists; for artifacts, essence precedes existence. A human being, having no maker with a plan, exists first and becomes something through choices; for people, existence precedes essence (Sartre, 1946). The distinction is exactly the one the agent literature has blurred. An agent is instantiated for a purpose, from a design, by a maker. For each run, a task and a capacity specification determine what the agent is meant to do and how it can act. That specification need not remain unchanged forever: a system can revise it before a later run.

TEC separates three questions: what should be accomplished (**Task**), where execution can observe and act (**Environment**), and how it selects and performs actions (**Capacity**). Binding these conditions defines the setup for an agent. The agent itself is the running execution unit, with a history that changes as it interacts with its environment. [D]

Four things follow at once, and they organize what comes after.

First, treating agents as interchangeable nodes hides the conditions under which each one acts. TEC starts with those conditions, then follows the execution they support. Section 4 uses this distinction to explain topology, tools, sub-agents, communication and memory.

Second, the task gives execution a direction. An unclear request can still start an agent, but it leaves the intended result uncertain. The specification failures discussed by Cemri et al. (2025) motivate clarifying that request; they do not imply that every task needs a large fixed schema.

Third, execution is limited by what the setup lets it distinguish. A team asked to review a proof cannot reliably distinguish a valid argument from a circular one if every member receives only the same conclusion and cannot inspect the derivation. Section 6.1 makes this information limit precise; the later deductions examine composition, execution plans and the joint value of Environment and Capacity.

Fourth, a system can use what happened during earlier runs to change how later runs work. A failed attempt might lead it to revise a prompt or add a reusable skill. If it saves that change and loads it for future tasks, it has updated its capacity. In TEC, this is the role of _evolve_. The update may help, do nothing, or make performance worse. To claim self-improvement, the system must compare the new version with the old one on tasks that were not used to produce the change, while accounting for extra cost and any abilities it has lost. Section 6.6 explains that comparison.

## 3. From a task to a running agent

Consider a theorem-proving request: “Prove that, for every integer n, if n² is even, then n is even.” The agent has access to definitions of even and odd integers, a workspace for its proof, and a way to request review. It can read the definitions, propose an argument, receive feedback about a missing step and revise the proof. TEC separates the conditions for this run from the interaction that unfolds during it.

### 3.1 Task: the requested outcome and its scope

A Task specifies what the execution is trying to accomplish. In the theorem-proving example, two pieces make the request specific:

- **The desired outcome:** establish that evenness of n² implies evenness of n. Without a target proposition, the agent has no direction for choosing an argument.
- **The relevant scope:** the claim concerns every integer n and requires a general proof. Checking several numerical cases does not satisfy that request; the domain and quantifier determine what the argument must establish.

These can be expressed in ordinary language. A structured record can help software manage the request, but a string is not inherently an inadequate task representation. The question is whether the request communicates the intended outcome clearly enough to act on.

A **verifier** is a procedure for judging a result against that outcome. Here, a reviewer can check whether each inference follows from the definitions and whether the argument covers every integer. Testing particular values can expose mistakes but cannot prove this universal claim. A formal proof checker is another option if the statement and argument are formalized; it is not required to begin the work. Some basis for judging success is needed to claim completion, but a separate automated verifier is not a prerequisite for a task or an agent.

Other information becomes useful when the system has a specific coordination problem:

- **Input/output contracts** matter at a handoff. A reviewing agent needs the exact statement, assumptions and proof draft, and should return either a justified assessment or a specific gap. A single agent keeping these together may not need separate contract fields.
- **Dependencies** matter after work is split. A review of a proposed proof depends on producing that draft. Recording this relation lets a scheduler avoid starting the dependent review too early; it is unnecessary for an undivided task.
- **A budget** matters when execution must stop at a resource limit. A time or token allowance tells the system when to stop searching, even if the proof remains incomplete. It is an execution constraint, not part of the proposition being proved.
- **Provenance** matters when results can be mixed up. If two agents produce different proofs, recording which draft a review examined prevents its approval from being attached to a different argument. It belongs in the execution record linked to the task.
- **Lifecycle status and failure attribution** serve different operational needs. Status tells a scheduler whether work is pending, running or finished, which helps avoid duplicate assignment. Failure attribution records an investigated explanation of what went wrong, such as an unjustified inference. Neither is needed to state the theorem, and an attribution field alone cannot identify a cause.

The minimum Task remains the requested outcome with the constraints needed to make it specific. Evaluation and coordination mechanisms should be added for the problems they solve, rather than made mandatory by definition.

### 3.2 Environment: what execution can observe and affect

For this run, the Environment is the proof workspace and the interfaces through which the agent reads definitions, records arguments and receives review. Two parts are needed:

- **State and responses:** the available definitions, current proof draft and feedback returned by a reviewer. Without them, the agent has no feedback about the argument it is developing.
- **An interaction boundary:** which material the agent may read and where it may write or request review. It can revise its proof draft, but it cannot change the requested theorem and count that as a proof of the original claim.

Choosing the environment does not freeze its state. Writing a new argument changes the proof workspace, and a later review examines that version. Other agents may also contribute lemmas or comments. The binding fixes which environment and access rules the run uses; the state encountered through them evolves.

A **view** names the part of the environment available to the agent. An **observation** is what it actually receives at a particular step. Access to a library of lemmas does not mean every lemma has been read. For an LLM agent, the **active context** is the input assembled for one model call, including the selected observations and relevant history. A **context policy** determines that selection. These distinctions explain why an available lemma can still be absent from a model's current input.

Large systems may keep additional working state in files or a persistent kernel. Such storage helps carry information across calls; it is an implementation choice, not another required layer in the minimum Environment definition.

### 3.3 Capacity: how the unit chooses and performs actions

Capacity describes the reusable means of acting. The proof attempt needs:

- **A way to select the next action** from the task, observations and execution history. In an LLM agent, a model and its instructions perform this role. Without it, access to definitions does not determine which proof strategy to try.
- **Available actions:** for this example, reading definitions, writing a proof step and requesting review. Without a way to record or communicate an argument, the unit cannot deliver the requested proof.

Capacity and Environment meet at an interface. Capacity determines which action the unit can select; the Environment determines what that action does to the current state and what result comes back. An operation for writing a proof step is part of the agent's action repertoire, while the recorded argument belongs to the Environment.

Prompts, reusable skills and tool implementations can supply this capacity. They are not each mandatory: a simple controller can act without a skill library. Similarly, a version number, a task signature or a performance record helps a system choose between capacities or evaluate updates; none is required for a capacity to exist. Those records become valuable when matching work to demonstrated ability, but their meaning depends on the environment in which ability was measured (Section 6.5).

### 3.4 Agent: the execution that unfolds under those conditions

Once the Task, Environment access and Capacity are bound, the system can instantiate an **Agent**, the execution unit that repeatedly observes and acts. Here, **runtime** refers to that running instance. The triple describes its setup; the Agent is the dynamic interaction process carried out under that setup. [D]

For the theorem, one possible run is:

1. The agent reads the definitions: an even integer has the form 2k, and an odd integer has the form 2k + 1, for an integer k.
2. It drafts “n² = 2k, therefore n is even” and submits the argument for review. Writing the draft changes the workspace.
3. The reviewer identifies the gap: the conclusion has been asserted without showing why it follows. Rewriting the hypothesis as n² = 2k does not supply that missing inference.
4. The agent uses this feedback to try the contrapositive. If n is odd, write n = 2m + 1. Then n² = 4m² + 4m + 1 = 2(2m² + 2m) + 1, which is odd because 2m² + 2m is an integer. Thus an odd integer cannot have an even square. Since every integer is either even or odd, an even square implies an even n.
5. The agent returns this complete argument for review. The reviewer can now inspect the parity classification and each algebraic step; if a gap remains, execution can continue or report the unresolved obligation.

The task can stay the same throughout this run, and so can the model and available tools. What changes is the environment, the accumulated history and the next action selected from them. Fixing the setup therefore does not fix the trajectory in advance. A single model call is one decision within this process, not the whole agent.

Multiple agents can start from the same setup and still have separate histories. Continuing an agent across a pause requires preserving enough execution state to resume its work; keeping only a reusable capacity starts another instance. This separates the means of acting from a particular ongoing execution.

### 3.5 Five operators organize execution

The observation–action loop describes how the agent runs. TEC uses five additional names to describe how a system organizes that execution:

- **generate** creates or refines a task request. In the example, it states the proposition, its integer domain and the requirement for a general proof.
- **match** selects a capacity and arranges the environment access needed to instantiate the execution unit.
- **assemble** selects observations and history for the next model call. It can run repeatedly as new feedback arrives.
- **verify** assesses a candidate result. A missing inference can send the running agent back to revise its proof; a complete argument can support acceptance. Verification does not precede every environmental change: the workspace already changed when the draft was written.
- **evolve** changes reusable capacity or configuration based on run records. It affects how later execution proceeds, and its benefits require the comparisons in Section 6.6.

These are organizational operations, not an exhaustive list of state transitions. They need not be five separate services or occur exactly once in a fixed sequence. In particular, assemble, action and feedback can repeat many times before a result is accepted.

## 4. Reinterpreting the objects of MAS research

MAS work often takes a topology, a team of roles, a communication protocol or a memory mechanism as its object of study. TEC asks what each of these changes in the execution of a task. The distinction matters for explaining results: a better score after changing a graph could come from a different division of work, different information reaching a model call, or different instructions at its nodes. Those are different interventions, even when one method packages them together. Related work on agent memory and automatic design helps identify mechanisms that a multi-agent system may also use; these mechanisms do not themselves require multiple agents.

### 4.1 Topology: which relation does an edge represent?

An agent topology is a graph that records a chosen relation between execution units. Before interpreting it, we must specify what its edges mean. “A must finish before B starts,” “B may read A's messages,” and “A may assign work to B” describe dependency, information access and control, respectively. They need not produce the same graph.

For the parity theorem, suppose one agent drafts the proof, two others review it, and a fourth prepares the final argument. One reviewer checks the algebraic expansion; the other checks the logical implication and integer assumptions. Both reviews need the draft, and finalization needs both reviews. Each review returns an annotated copy of the draft, so the finalizer receives the argument together with the findings. If an edge means **the next unit needs this unit's output**, the graph has four edges: Draft → Check A, Draft → Check B, Check A → Finalize, and Check B → Finalize.

TEC explains the design decisions behind that drawing:

- **Task:** the proof request has been split into drafting, two review obligations and finalization. This decomposition creates the dependencies.
- **Environment:** the reviewers can read the draft, and the finalizer can read their findings. Permission to read a result makes the handoff possible; **assemble** determines which parts actually enter a model call.
- **Capacity and operator policy:** **match** assigns capacities to these requests. Instructions can specialize one reviewer in algebra and the other in logic. The coordination policy decides who may request another review or accept the result through **verify**.

Now keep all four edges and change the reviewers. They might use identical instructions and receive the whole draft, or use different instructions and receive material selected for their review obligations. The handoff graph stays the same, but the computations and available evidence differ. Conversely, letting Check B inspect Check A's findings adds a dependency if B must wait for them. That changes both the information available to B and the opportunity for parallel execution.

This is the precise sense in which topology is a **projection** in TEC: select one relation from a fuller execution design and retain its nodes and edges. The graph is useful, but it omits the task content, model instructions, selected observations and other relations unless these are explicitly attached as annotations. [D] The three primitives alone do not determine a unique graph; decomposition, matching and access policies must also be specified.

This distinction also prevents misreading graph-based research. In [GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16823v3), nodes represent operations such as model calls, rather than necessarily whole agents, and edges represent information flow. The method optimizes node prompts and graph connectivity. TEC reads these as changes to Capacity and to the routing of information into subsequent execution. A gain from their joint optimization cannot be attributed to graph shape alone. A useful comparison therefore records what changed at the nodes and what information changed at the edges, alongside the resulting cost and success rate.

### 4.2 Roles and hierarchies: who does which work, with what authority?

A role name such as “prover,” “critic” or “orchestrator” can conceal several different choices. Assigning a critic the obligation to check the parity argument changes its **Task**. Giving it instructions for checking quantifiers changes its **Capacity**. Giving it the proof draft changes its **Environment**. Allowing it to reject the result or request another attempt assigns authority over **verify** or **generate**. Calling it a critic does not tell us which of these choices was made. [D]

Existing systems combine these choices in different ways. [MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352v7) encodes standard operating procedures and uses structured outputs for handoffs. TEC reads its organization as task decomposition plus rules for what downstream executions receive. [Magentic-One (Fourney et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04468v1) gives an Orchestrator responsibility for planning, tracking progress and replanning while directing specialized agents. TEC locates the hierarchy in that allocation of coordination decisions, together with the capacities available to perform the assigned work.

A sub-agent called through a tool interface fits the same account. When the prover requests a review, the request is an action from the caller's perspective. The child is still a complete execution unit with its own task, observations and action sequence. “Tool” describes the calling interface; “agent” describes the execution behind it. Whether that child persists is another design choice.

The research question becomes more specific than which role names form the best team: does a gain come from specialized instructions, a better division of obligations, different evidence, or a better allocation of coordination decisions?

### 4.3 Communication: how does one execution's result reach another?

Communication research studies what agents exchange, who receives it and when it arrives. TEC represents this through changes to the Environment and the assembly of later inputs. A message must be recorded or delivered somewhere, become observable to its recipient, and then be used in a decision. These are separate steps; making a message readable does not guarantee that it enters the next model call. [D]

In the proof example, a reviewer might send “the implication after n² = 2k is unjustified” through an inbox or attach it to the proof draft. Either can give the prover the same observation. Sending only “incorrect” delivers less useful evidence even if the graph and number of messages are unchanged. Giving both reviewers access to each other's comments also changes their information, potentially sacrificing independent assessment.

MetaGPT's structured handoffs provide one concrete example of communication whose content and format are part of the design. The older blackboard and tuple-space approaches likewise organize how partial results become available to other problem solvers (Erman et al., 1980; Gelernter, 1985). TEC groups these mechanisms by the information they expose and the rules governing its use, rather than by whether the interface looks like chat or shared storage.

This representation does not make implementations equivalent in cost. An inbox and a shared document may differ in delivery delay, ordering, concurrency and retrieval overhead. The comparison should ask whether they deliver the needed evidence at the needed step, at what cost, and with what effect on the resulting proof. Section 6.1 derives the limit that appears when this process loses a distinction the task requires.

### 4.4 Memory: what is retained, and how does it affect later execution?

Memory research concerns both the information retained from earlier activity and the mechanism that makes it useful later. [MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560v2) manages movement between memory tiers and the limited model context. [A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12110v11) organizes notes with attributes and links, and updates existing notes as new material arrives. These approaches change more than the amount stored: they change how information is organized and made available.

TEC separates three interventions:

- **Retain information in the Environment.** Save the parity proof, its assumptions and the reviewer’s objection. A later execution can consult that record without repeating the same discovery.
- **Change retrieval and assemble.** Index the record under parity or retrieve it when an argument needs an odd/even case split. A saved proof cannot help a model call that never receives the relevant part.
- **Adopt a reusable procedure in Capacity.** Turn experience into an instruction such as “identify the justification for each implication before submitting a proof,” and load it as part of how later runs act. This changes the reusable means of reasoning, rather than merely adding another fact to consult.

[Agent Workflow Memory (Wang et al., 2024c)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07429v1) illustrates procedural reuse: it derives reusable workflows from past experiences and supplies them to guide later tasks. In TEC, storing such a workflow concerns the Environment; retrieving it concerns **assemble**; adopting it as a reusable action procedure can change Capacity. The mapping depends on how the system uses it, not on the word “memory” in its name. [D]

These distinctions identify what an evaluation should isolate. Did the system improve because it retained a useful lemma, retrieved it at the right moment, or changed its proof strategy? Persistence across runs alone does not answer that question. Nor does every new observation constitute a capacity update: feedback on the current draft can change the next action while the model, instructions and available actions stay fixed.

### 4.5 Workflow search and self-improvement: what is being optimized?

Automatic agent-design methods search different parts of the execution setup. [DSPy (Khattab et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03714v1) compiles declarative language-model programs and optimizes their components against a metric. GPTSwarm searches prompts and connectivity. [ADAS (Hu et al., 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08435v2) uses a meta-agent to propose agent code, evaluates candidates and maintains an archive of designs. Their common object is a configurable procedure for solving tasks, but their search spaces are different.

TEC describes a candidate by the choices it changes: task-generation rules, matching, context assembly, the capacities used, or the procedure for evaluating results. Search proposes changes to some of these choices; evaluation determines which candidates are retained. When an accepted change becomes reusable configuration, that is the operation called **evolve**. This description separates the object being optimized from the optimizer that proposes the change. [D]

For theorem proving, one search might change only the prover's instructions. Another might introduce separate algebra and logic reviews. A third might change which lemmas are retrieved. All could improve the final proof, but they intervene on different parts of TEC and incur different costs. Calling all three “a better agent” hides the explanation that the experiment should establish.

The same distinction clarifies self-improvement. An external search process can optimize an agent design. A system claiming to improve itself must also show how its own execution records inform changes that later runs actually use. Neither process guarantees improvement merely by producing a new configuration. Section 6.6 asks whether the retained change helps on tasks that did not guide its selection, and whether the gain survives accounting for cost and regressions.

## 5. Where the framework comes from

TEC is a synthesis; each part has a history in a field that studied coordination before language models did, and each lineage contributes one lesson. Distributed AI—the contract net (Smith, 1980), the blackboard (Erman et al., 1980; Nii, 1986), Linda (Gelernter, 1985), the Dec-POMDP result (Bernstein et al., 2002), multi-robot task allocation (Gerkey and Matarić, 2004)—contributed the object model and the lesson that organization was never studied apart from task and information structure. Organization theory and economics—Simon (1955), Galbraith (1974), Malone and Crowston (1994), Coase (1937), Williamson (1975), Wellman (1993)—contributed the demand-and-supply view and the lesson that the choice between planning and matching is a function of task uncertainty, dependency and communication cost. Ensemble theory and test-time compute—Krogh and Vedelsby (1995), Hong and Page (2004), self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), pass@k (Chen et al., 2021), the repeated-sampling laws of Brown et al. (2024) and Snell et al. (2024)—contributed the lesson that homogeneous repetition is a known baseline that must be controlled alongside diversity and assembly policy. Evolutionary computation and continual learning—variation and selection (Holland, 1975), novelty and quality-diversity (Lehman and Stanley, 2011; Mouret and Clune, 2015), open-endedness (Wang et al., 2019; Hughes et al., 2024), forgetting and negative transfer (McCloskey and Cohen, 1989; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Pan and Yang, 2010)—contributed the lesson that improvement is a claim about the future and must be validated on tasks the update did not see. What TEC adds is only the assembly, around the specific properties of language-model agents: cheap instantiation, prompt-defined capacity, token-priced compute, and an active context that carries selected observations and history into each model call.

## 6. What TEC implies about the limits and sources of improvement

An agent's result depends on the interaction of its task, the information and actions its environment permits, and the capacity operating on them. This has consequences that cannot be read from agent count or graph shape alone. The deductions below make their assumptions explicit. Their logical ingredients are familiar; TEC connects them to particular choices of **generate**, **match**, **assemble**, **verify** and **evolve**.

### 6.1 More computation cannot recover a distinction that every view removes

Consider two submissions of the parity proof. One contains the valid contrapositive argument from Section 3.4. The other assumes n is even and then presents that assumption as its conclusion. The review task is to assess the submitted argument, not to produce a new proof of a theorem already known to be true.

Suppose **assemble** sends the reviewer only the last line, “therefore n is even,” and no operation available during the review can recover the preceding argument. The visible input is identical, but the correct verdicts differ. A larger model, more reviewers or further debate over that same input cannot reliably tell which submission it came from.

The deduction is direct. Fix the capacities, policies and any prior information. Assume the initial joint information available to the whole team is identical, including private memory and relevant model priors. Further assume that, after any shared visible history, every permitted action returns the same conditional observation distribution in the two cases. The next action then has the same distribution too. Applying that argument at each step gives the same distribution over final verdicts. For an equally likely pair requiring opposite binary verdicts, average accuracy is at most one half. [D] Extra random samples can change which verdict is selected, but cannot correlate it with the hidden difference.

This is a limit on **missing evidence**, not on reasoning from evidence that is present. More computation can uncover a consequence of a visible argument. Retrieval can reveal a previously hidden premise. A model may have relevant prior knowledge. Each can matter; the bound applies only while the cases remain indistinguishable through every permitted source of information.

The TEC consequence is that some apparent Capacity failures are actually failures of the Task–Environment binding. If the task requires distinguishing two cases that the available views collapse together, replacing or replicating the capacity cannot solve that defect. The intervention must preserve the distinguishing evidence, permit an action that obtains it, or narrow the task. A communication protocol or summary should therefore be assessed by which task-relevant distinctions it preserves, not just by how many tokens it saves.

### 6.2 Decomposition creates a new obligation: local successes must compose

Suppose two child tasks return correct statements:

- If n is even, then n² is even.
- If n² is odd, then n is odd.

Both are true, but they are contrapositives of each other. Neither supplies the requested direction, “if n² is even, then n is even.” A finalizer cannot establish that direction merely by collecting these two successful outputs. It still needs another argument.

This exposes a distinction between **verify** at a child task and **verify** at the parent. Assume each local check is sound for its assigned claim. Even then, all those checks can pass even when **generate** chose claims that do not establish the parent goal. A decomposition supports a correctness guarantee only if its composition rule also guarantees that accepted child results, with their assumptions satisfied, establish the parent task. [D] This is the familiar requirement of sound composition, applied to the way a multi-agent system creates and combines work.

For the parity theorem, a suitable child could instead establish that every odd integer has an odd square. The finalizer can then use the fact that every integer is either even or odd to derive the requested implication. The child result needs to retain its domain and quantifier. Replacing it with “parity check passed” hides the information needed to justify that composition.

Thus delegation can move reasoning into the handoff and integration stages rather than remove it. A short output can reduce transmission cost while increasing integration cost when it omits evidence the next task needs. The parent must then reconstruct the missing argument or accept an unsupported result. More locally successful agents can therefore increase the amount of integration work without increasing coverage of the original goal.

The useful unit of decomposition is consequently an obligation whose accepted result can be used by its successors. This does not require every task to carry a formal certificate. It requires the strength of the system's overall correctness claim to match the strength of its local checks and its composition procedure.

### 6.3 A task has no single critical path independent of Capacity and Environment

For a fixed execution plan D represented by an acyclic dependency graph of serial operations, with fixed durations and p identical workers, completion time is bounded below by

$$
\tau(D,p) \geq \max\left(\frac{W_D}{p},\,L_D\right),
$$

where $W_D$ is the total work and $L_D$ is the longest dependency chain. This is a scheduling bound. It says what adding workers can achieve **after the plan has been chosen**.

The theorem to prove does not uniquely determine that plan. With only definitions available, a prover may need to construct the odd-square argument, check its algebra and justify the final implication. If the Environment supplies a trusted proof of the odd-square lemma, and the Capacity can interpret and apply it, another plan can retrieve the lemma, check that its assumptions match and apply the contrapositive. The target Task stays the same, but the operations and dependencies change.

It follows that the achievable parallelism is a property of a feasible **Task–Environment–Capacity plan**, not of the task statement alone. [D] More workers cannot shorten $L_D$ while D stays fixed. Changing the available lemmas, the action repertoire or the decomposition can replace D with a plan having a different span. Retrieval and checking costs belong in that new plan; reuse is not automatically faster.

This also changes how scaling results should be interpreted. If a system replans when more agents become available, its speedup combines a scheduling effect with a change in the work performed. If a system cannot discover a useful decomposition, adding execution capacity can leave its main constraint untouched. Centralized and decentralized generation are alternative ways to find plans; TEC does not establish that either wins without assumptions about discovery, communication and verification costs.

### 6.4 Shared results can improve the system without improving any individual Capacity

Take a sequence of proof tasks that reuse the parity lemma. In the first run, an agent derives the lemma and records a checked argument together with its assumptions. A later run can consult that result instead of deriving it again. The model, instructions and available actions can all remain unchanged.

The causal sequence is **verify → retain in Environment → assemble for a later task → generate a different plan**. Section 6.3 explains why the final step matters: reusable information can eliminate the need to regenerate an argument and its intermediate artifacts. If the later run substitutes reuse for rederivation, and the result is sound, applicable and cheaper to retrieve and check, it reduces the work spent on that obligation while Capacity stays fixed. [D] Overall latency may remain unchanged if another dependency chain is the limiting one.

The improvement belongs to the system's accumulated environment. It need not belong to the agent that produced the result, and it is not limited to that agent's future runs. Another execution unit can benefit if its view exposes the result and its capacity can use it. This gives a reason to organize shared knowledge around reusable mathematical claims and their conditions of application, rather than only around the conversations of individual agents.

It also separates three explanations for a better score. A later system may possess more useful results, retrieve the same results more effectively, or reason better given the same information. These correspond to different changes in TEC. If an evaluation resets all environmental state between tasks, it measures performance without cross-task accumulation. If it retains the state, a gain cannot by itself be attributed to a stronger Capacity.

The converse matters too: more stored results need not help. Inapplicable lemmas, unsupported claims or irrelevant retrieval can add work or mislead execution. The deduction concerns a usable, checked result under the stated cost condition; the practical question is how often an accumulated result meets those conditions on later tasks.

### 6.5 Improving Environment and Capacity separately can miss their joint value

Suppose a proof task specifically requires a machine-checked derivation. Consider a restricted system that can produce prose but cannot translate it into a formal proof within its budget. Its environment initially contains only a prose argument.

Adding an action that submits formal proofs to a checker does not complete the task: no suitable formal proof is available. Supplying a valid formal proof file while leaving that submission action unavailable does not obtain a checker result either. Together, the file and the action can allow the system to complete the task. This example assumes the two missing steps cannot be supplied by another route within the allowed budget.

The two interventions are complements. Evaluating either only against the original system can assign it no immediate success benefit, even though their combination has value. A search that rejects every change without an immediate gain can therefore reject both components of a useful redesign. The cost of producing the formal artifact must also be counted when evaluating the redesign end to end. Consequently, there is no general rule that optimizing Capacity at a fixed Environment, then optimizing Environment at a fixed Capacity, finds the best joint setup. [D] The same issue arises when a specialized reasoning procedure needs a particular representation, or a retrieved lemma becomes useful only to a capacity that knows how to apply it.

This makes **match** more than selecting the highest-ranked model for a task label. It selects a capacity together with the observations, representations and actions through which that capacity can do the work. Performance records from one view or interface may not rank capacities correctly under another. A context policy is likewise evaluated relative to the capacity consuming its output.

For research, the implication is a joint comparison: hold the task family and budget fixed, and compare both versions of Capacity with both versions of Environment. Changing one at a time is useful for diagnosis, but testing only the two isolated changes can miss a benefit that appears only when they are combined. Whether such complementarities dominate a particular MAS application remains empirical.

### 6.6 A self-modifying system can change the meaning of its own score

The **evolve** operator may change Capacity, but it can also change rules for generating tasks, selecting context or verifying results. This creates an identification problem: an increase in the system's reported success rate need not mean that its capacity to solve the original tasks improved.

For example, a prover might revise its reasoning instructions after submitting an unjustified implication. But a system that also controls task generation could make its workload easier, repeatedly requesting lemmas it already knows. A system that changes verification could begin accepting “the conclusion is true” in place of an assessment of the submitted proof. Both changes can raise the fraction of tasks marked successful without making the original reasoning procedure better.

In TEC terms, the observed score is produced by the interaction of the generated Tasks, the supplied Environment, the chosen Capacities and the verification policy. If several change together, the score difference alone does not identify which change caused it. [D] Agreement among updated agents does not resolve this: their agreement is another system output, not an independent standard of proof correctness.

A comparison must therefore keep a stable meaning for success. To measure a Capacity change, evaluate old and new versions on the same held-out task distribution, with comparable environmental resources, acceptance criteria and budgets. To measure improvement of the whole system, allow its internal organization to change while fixing the external task objective and assessment, and include the cost of obtaining and maintaining its accumulated state. Then vary components separately where attribution matters.

This permits internal verifiers and task generators to improve. They need not remain frozen in deployment. But their revisions must be assessed against an external criterion that is not silently relaxed by the candidate being evaluated. Otherwise the system can optimize what gets counted as success instead of becoming better at the task. Section 8.2 turns this consequence into a concrete evaluation procedure.

## 7. Limits of the framework

**The boundary between state and configuration needs to be explicit.** Section 4.4 distinguishes retained information, its retrieval, and reusable procedures that change action selection. Systems that edit their own prompts or skills during a run can cross that boundary. Comparing such runs requires recording when the configuration changed; the initial triple alone does not describe the whole history.

**Three regions fit poorly.** Social simulation—Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) and descendants—is in TEC terms the most thorough study of an environment that exists (memory stream as view, reflection as policy), but it is organized around believability rather than task success; the framework can describe it and cannot evaluate it. Adversarial and mixed-motive settings assume agents whose essence is not the task they were assigned; TEC assumes cooperation and needs extension before it can speak there. And learning that changes model weights rather than harness state is an evolve operation on a different timescale that the framework does not yet distinguish.

**Two assumptions are probably false.** That every runtime attempts its assigned task and reports honestly: multi-agent risk research shows this fails under injected instructions that propagate between runtimes and under correlated failures no single runtime would produce (Hammond et al., 2025). And that capacities are stationary, when models are replaced on a cadence of months and an update can invalidate every record attached to a prompt built for its predecessor; any empirical constant must be pinned to a model version.

**A minimal model leaves operational questions open.** Access rules do not by themselves specify how to handle adversarial instructions or concurrent writes. Human judgment may supply task clarification and acceptance, but TEC does not model that collaboration in detail. Noisy evaluation, persistent teams and changing deadlines also need explicit treatment when they matter. These are limits on what can be concluded from the minimal model, rather than reasons to place every such concern inside the Task definition.

**Repeated updates need evaluation over time.** A new configuration still precedes each run, even when it was produced from earlier runs. There is no contradiction between a designed agent and a system that changes its design. The unresolved question is how to evaluate a long sequence of such updates: whether early gains persist, whether later changes undo earlier ones, and whether accumulated update costs remain worthwhile. Passing one comparison does not settle those questions for the entire sequence.

## 8. How to test the implications

Programmable harnesses make it possible to vary these parts separately. The recursive-language-model design of Zhang, Kraska and Khattab (2025a) treats the prompt as data in an external environment that the model can inspect. Prime Intellect's harness (2026) provides persistent kernel state, child runtimes and editable prompts and skills. Such mechanisms can support experiments on retained information, context assembly and configuration changes. Their availability is not evidence that a particular change improves performance.

### 8.1 Separate information, organization and computation

A theorem-proving evaluation can distinguish the deductions by changing one relationship at a time:

| Implication                      | Controlled comparison                                                                                                                     | What the result distinguishes                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Information limits (6.1)         | Give reviewers either identical summaries of valid and circular drafts or the full arguments; then vary inference compute.                | Missing evidence versus insufficient reasoning about visible evidence.                                   |
| Composition (6.2)                | Keep child-result correctness fixed while varying whether the returned claims and assumptions establish the parent obligation.            | Local success versus a justified final proof.                                                            |
| Plan-dependent parallelism (6.3) | Sweep worker count under a fixed plan, then separately permit replanning.                                                                 | Scheduling gains versus changes in the work performed.                                                   |
| Shared accumulation (6.4)        | Freeze Capacity and compare an empty store, a checked lemma store and irrelevant retained material. Include retrieval and checking costs. | Useful environmental accumulation versus a stronger reasoning procedure or indiscriminate extra context. |
| Complementarity (6.5)            | Evaluate both Capacity versions with both Environment versions under the same budget.                                                     | Benefits that occur only when the two changes are combined.                                              |
| Evaluation stability (6.6)       | Compare internal success reports with a fixed external assessment before and after a configuration update.                                | Better task performance versus changed task selection or acceptance.                                     |

Repeated sampling remains an important control. With identical capacities and active contexts, independent calls produce the same candidate distribution as repeated calls to one model. A selector restricted to choosing an existing candidate cannot succeed unless the set contains a correct answer; a synthesizing aggregator is not subject to that candidate-selection bound and its computation must be counted (Chen et al., 2021). Multi-round debate needs a richer baseline: a single process can reproduce its output distribution only if it preserves participant histories and matches each call's conditional distribution, tools, observations, scheduling and aggregation. Sequential simulation need not match parallel latency. [D] These controls prevent improvements due to extra computation from being credited to organization alone.

### 8.2 Evaluate an update against a stable task objective

For a prover that changes its instructions after an unjustified inference, use the following procedure:

1. Record the changed component and the runs or feedback that led to the change.
2. Confirm that later runs actually load the saved version. A correction confined to the current conversation does not establish a persistent update.
3. Compare old and new versions on held-out proof tasks that did not guide generation or selection of the update, using the same external assessment and comparable budgets.
4. Run repeated attempts and report both success and cost. Include the cost of generating, evaluating and maintaining the update or its supporting store.
5. Check previously successful tasks for regressions. If the Environment changed too, compare versions under controlled store and retrieval conditions to identify the source of the gain.
6. Restore individual components where possible to test attribution. A gain from a prompt, retrieval policy and verifier changed together is not automatically a prompt gain.
7. Keep the previous version and a way to restore it. Rollback makes adoption reversible; it is not evidence of improvement.

The Darwin Gödel Machine (Zhang et al., 2025e), with its archive of agent-code versions, illustrates why version retention and evidence of improvement are separate questions. The archive establishes that candidates and earlier versions remain available. Their benefits, costs, transfer and causal explanation require evaluation. These checks operationalize Section 6.6; they are an experimental procedure rather than additional primitives or a theorem that every useful update must satisfy all seven to work.

## 9. Closing

Task specifies the intended outcome, Environment supplies the state and interaction boundary, and Capacity supplies the means of selecting and performing actions. Once these are bound, an agent is the execution unit that repeatedly observes, acts and uses feedback. Its setup can remain stable while its history and environment change.

Improving a multi-agent system therefore requires more than choosing its graph or increasing its population. The available evidence must support the distinctions the task demands; local results must support the claims their successors make; and a capacity must be evaluated with the environment through which it acts. Changing those relations can improve the system even when no individual model changes. Demonstrating that improvement requires an objective that remains comparable across the versions being judged.
